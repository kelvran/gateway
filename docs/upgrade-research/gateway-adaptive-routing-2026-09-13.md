# Adaptive / Learned Model Routing for the Gateway — Deep Research (2026-09-13)

## Question

Kelvran's `gateway/internal/router/` implements only static/rule-based routing today: weighted round-robin (`wrr.go`), cost-tier-aware selection (`cost_tier.go`), health-based circuit breaking (`health.go`), a post-recovery weight ramp (`recovery_ramp.go`), and a sample window (`samplewindow.go`). No learned, classifier-based, or bandit-based routing exists anywhere in the codebase. What does current (2026) production practice for adaptive LLM routing actually look like — RouteLLM-style classifier routing, Martian/NotDiamond/Unify-style commercial routers, and bandit algorithms (Thompson sampling, UCB, contextual bandits) for online cost-quality-latency tradeoff optimization — and which parts, if any, are validated production deployments versus still-research-stage academic proposals? Specifically: could Kelvran's own already-shipped operational signals (per-deployment cost tiers, health/latency samples in `samplewindow.go`) feed a lightweight adaptive router without a new ML training pipeline?

## Context (Kelvran's own real, current state)

Everything in `gateway/internal/router/` today is deterministic and rule-based: smooth weighted round-robin over deployments, a discrete cost-tier filter, N-of-M health thresholds with backoff, a linear post-recovery ramp, and a rolling sample window for health/latency observations. There is no query-complexity signal, no learned win-probability model, no preference dataset, and no bandit-style probabilistic arm selection anywhere in the router. This report evaluates whether *learned* (classifier- or bandit-based) adaptive routing — as opposed to the static rules already shipped — is ready to build now, and if so, what the lowest-risk version looks like against Kelvran's existing signals.

## Summary

The dominant *research-stage* pattern for adaptive routing is RouteLLM-style classifier routing (matrix factorization, BERT, causal-LLM, or similarity-weighted ranking) trained on a labeled human-preference dataset (Chatbot Arena battles plus GPT-4-judge augmentation) — a real, shipped open-source project with strong benchmark numbers, but calibrated only for open-ended chat quality, not agent/tool-calling task success, and shown by a large-scale 2026 benchmark (400K+ instances, 33 models) to frequently fail to beat simply picking one best fixed model, with "model-recall failure" (not miscalibrated cost/quality weighting) the dominant driver of the remaining gap to an oracle router. Bandit-based approaches (contextual bandits, Thompson Sampling, LinUCB) are architecturally more promising for Kelvran because they need only operational reward feedback (success/cost/latency) rather than a labeled preference dataset — but the academic bandit-routing literature found here (PILOT, OrcaRouter) is evaluated only via offline simulation on academic benchmarks, not live production traffic. The one confirmed, real, production deployment of adaptive routing in this research is Ramp's Thompson-Sampling router, shipped to real traffic on a reranker use case and a streaming use case (Ramp Inspect), now generalized into a commercial product (Router.com) — and it is architecturally the closest precedent to what Kelvran could build directly on top of its own already-shipped cost-tier and sample-window signals, without any new labeled-data pipeline.

## Findings

### Finding 1 — RouteLLM is a real, shipped OSS classifier-based adaptive router, but every one of its non-random router types requires a labeled human-preference dataset as a hard prerequisite (confidence: high)

RouteLLM (`lm-sys/RouteLLM`, Apache-2.0, pip-installable, ~5.5k stars) ships four trained/tuned routers — matrix factorization, a BERT classifier, an LLM-based (causal) classifier, and similarity-weighted ranking — all learned from data rather than static rules, demonstrating that classifier-based adaptive routing between a strong and a weak model is a real, deployed OSS pattern, not purely theoretical. Every one of these routers, however, is trained or tuned on the same underlying preference dataset: 80k human-judged Chatbot Arena battles, optionally augmented with a purpose-built GPT-4-as-judge dataset of ~120K samples (built at a cost of ~$700). None of this training data includes operational signals like cost or latency — cost/latency are applied post-hoc via a separately calibrated threshold at inference time, not as training inputs. On MT-Bench, the matrix-factorization router (Arena data + LLM-judge augmentation) reached 95% of GPT-4's quality while routing only 14% of prompts to GPT-4, reported by the authors as ~75% cheaper than a 50/50-swept random baseline and over 85% cheaper than always calling GPT-4 — but this is a 2024 academic/MT-Bench benchmark result, not evidence of large-scale validated production deployment.

**Build now / not yet: not_yet.** Trigger: a labeled query-complexity or preference/comparison dataset at real volume — exactly the prerequisite Kelvran does not have. Kelvran's own evals/judge-panel corpus is the natural future source of such labels, but does not yet exist at the coverage needed to train a router of this type.

### Finding 2 — RouteLLM-style classifiers are calibrated for open-ended chat preference, not agent/tool-task success, and exhibit a documented safety fragility: they mis-route by query *category* rather than true complexity (confidence: high)

Every RouteLLM router is calibrated to predict human preference between open-ended chat responses — not whether a structured agent task (tool calls, schema validity) will succeed. A peer-reviewed EACL 2026 paper (Kassem, Schölkopf, Jin) found the most commonly cited learned-router architecture (BERT-based, RouteLLM-style) directs *all* coding and math queries to the most powerful LLM — even when a simpler model would suffice — while routing jailbreak/adversarial attempts to weaker, less-aligned models, elevating safety risk. In other words, the router is keying on query *category*, not genuine per-query complexity, which is precisely the kind of misclassification that would matter for any gateway serving mixed workloads including sensitive or adversarial traffic.

**Build now / not yet: not_yet**, and flag as a standing caution even if this pattern is ever pursued later: validate routing accuracy on Kelvran's own task mix (not benchmark numbers) before trusting a classifier router with anything security- or safety-adjacent, and expect category-based failure modes rather than assuming smooth complexity-based degradation.

### Finding 3 — Cascade routing (FrugalGPT-style: call cheapest model first, escalate via a scoring function) is a distinct, real academic mechanism with strong reported numbers, but is self-reported and academic-benchmark-only (confidence: high)

FrugalGPT's cascade approach calls models sequentially from cheapest to most expensive, stopping when a scoring function accepts the answer, and reports up to 98% cost reduction versus always using the strongest model while matching its performance — a genuine, distinct architecture from RouteLLM's single upfront routing decision. This is architecturally different from Kelvran's existing deterministic, error-classified fallback chain (which escalates on *failure*, not on a quality-scoring function), so it isn't redundant with what's shipped — but the reported number is a self-reported academic benchmark figure on specific NLP datasets, not an independently audited or production-validated outcome.

**Build now / not yet: not_yet.** The scoring-function-driven escalation still requires a calibrated acceptance threshold per task type, which needs the same kind of labeled quality data Kelvran doesn't yet have; Kelvran's existing fallback chain already covers the "escalate on hard failure" case with zero additional data requirements.

### Finding 4 — Bandit-based routing (contextual bandits, Thompson Sampling, LinUCB) needs only operational reward feedback, not labeled preference data — but the academic instances found here are offline-simulation-only, not live production (confidence: high)

Two academic systems frame LLM routing explicitly as a bandit problem. PILOT (EMNLP 2025 Findings) formulates routing as a contextual multi-armed bandit — a novel preference-prior-informed extension of LinUCB — learning solely from binary bandit feedback (was the chosen model's response good or not) rather than requiring supervised query-to-optimal-model labels across all models, which is architecturally exactly the kind of signal Kelvran could source from its own operational data (success/failure, cost, latency) without a new labeled dataset. However, PILOT's own evaluation is explicitly offline: the authors state they are "the first to study LLM routing in an online bandit learning setting" but construct that setting synthetically, via a simulated replay of the RouterBench/MMLU dataset split into "learning" and "deployment" buckets — not a live system with real multi-model traffic. Separately, OrcaRouter is described in its own paper as "production-oriented," but its reported results come from the public RouterArena benchmark leaderboard (8,400 evaluation queries, evaluated frozen), not live production traffic with real users — the paper's production language should be read as an architectural design goal, not a validated live-traffic result.

**Build now / not yet: not_yet** for these specific academic systems as shipped research artifacts (offline-simulation-only, no production precedent) — **but the underlying principle (bandit feedback only, no labeled dataset required) is the key enabling fact for Finding 6 below.**

### Finding 5 — Large-scale 2026 benchmarking shows most learned/classifier routers — including a widely-deployed commercial router — fail to reliably beat a simple best-single-model baseline, with "model-recall failure" as the dominant driver of the remaining gap to an oracle router (confidence: high)

A large, peer-reviewed unified benchmark (LLMRouterBench, ACL Findings 2026: 400K+ instances, 33 models, 21 datasets, 10 routing baselines including commercial routers) found that most learned/classifier-based routing methods — including the commercial router OpenRouter — fail to reliably outperform a simple "Best Single model" baseline, either on accuracy or on cost-without-accuracy-loss; OpenRouter specifically showed a −24.7% performance change relative to Best Single despite operating over a much larger candidate pool. The same benchmark found the dominant driver of the remaining gap to an oracle router is "model-recall failure": current routers (embedding/classifier-based) frequently fail to identify the one or few candidate models that can actually answer a query correctly, even when only the top-3 candidates need to include it (recall of only 46–51% at k=3 for two tested baselines). A separate, independent statistical paper (arXiv 2608.08265) formally certified that the theoretical oracle-vs-best-fixed-model opportunity gap is large (9.7–30.7 percentage points depending on task) but that real, trained routers realize only a small fraction of it — the best router in that study recovered just 7.5–14.4% of the certified oracle gain, and the simultaneous confidence interval for the best of eleven tested routing policies had a lower bound of zero throughout (statistically indistinguishable from no improvement).

**Build now / not yet: not_yet — and this is the single most load-bearing finding in this report.** Classifier-based routing is not yet a mature, reliably-beneficial production pattern in general, independent of whether Kelvran has training data or not; the bottleneck the literature identifies (model-recall failure, not cost/quality miscalibration) is not something a router built from Kelvran's own signals would automatically solve either.

### Finding 6 — The one confirmed, real, production deployment of adaptive (bandit-based) routing found in this research operates purely on operational reward signals — the same class of signal Kelvran already collects (confidence: high, single company source but externally corroborated)

Ramp has shipped a production, Thompson-Sampling-based LLM router to real traffic — not a research prototype — first on a reranker use case with a tight latency deadline (with a documented live incident where rerouting kicked in during a Gemini rate-limiting event), then extended to a streaming use case (Ramp Inspect, ~30% cost savings), and now generalized into a commercial product, Router.com. This system is corroborated independently via Hacker News discussion and a third-party tech article confirming the product's existence, timeline, and headline ~30% internal cost-cut figure (the specific Thompson Sampling mechanism itself is not independently confirmed beyond Ramp's own blog, but the product and outcome are). Architecturally, this is the class of system closest to what Kelvran could build: a bandit reward signal built from operational data (success, cost, latency) requiring no labeled preference dataset and no offline training pipeline — directly analogous to PILOT's "bandit feedback only" formulation (Finding 4), but with an actual production deployment behind it rather than an offline simulation.

**Build now / not yet: build_now-adjacent — this is the pattern to imitate, not adopt wholesale.** No trigger needed to *start* prototyping; Kelvran already collects the two ingredients this pattern needs (per-deployment cost tiers via `cost_tier.go`, rolling success/latency samples via `samplewindow.go`). What's missing is not data but a policy layer: a Thompson-Sampling (or simple epsilon-greedy) arm-selection step that treats each `samplewindow.go`-tracked deployment as a bandit arm and uses recent success/cost/latency as the reward, re-weighting the existing WRR candidate pool instead of replacing it. This is architecturally a much smaller lift than any RouteLLM-style classifier and does not require a new ML training pipeline — but it is genuinely new algorithmic surface (probabilistic arm selection, reward-signal design, exploration/exploitation tuning) that should go through Kelvran's own design/RFC process before being treated as a drop-in win, and it inherits the same traffic-volume caveat already named for Kelvran's health/circuit-breaking work: bandit exploration needs enough real request volume per deployment to converge, which is an open question at Kelvran's current traffic level (see Open Questions).

## Caveats

- Two systems in this research (OrcaRouter, PILOT) use "production-oriented" framing or claim to study routing "in an online bandit setting," but their actual reported results are benchmark-leaderboard or offline-simulation results, not live-traffic validation. Read any router paper's "production" language skeptically and check what data the reported numbers actually came from.
- RouteLLM's headline MT-Bench numbers (95% GPT-4 quality at 14% call rate, 75–85% cost reduction) are a 2024 academic benchmark result on a conversational benchmark, not a production-validated outcome, and several more sweeping cost/quality claims about RouteLLM (e.g. ">2x cost reduction with no quality loss," "40% fewer large-model calls at <5% degradation on agent tasks") were checked adversarially in this research pass and did **not** survive verification — treat any RouteLLM savings figure encountered elsewhere with the same skepticism until traced to its exact benchmark and comparison baseline.
- The large-scale benchmark findings (LLMRouterBench, the oracle-gap statistical paper) are very recent 2026 publications; the oracle-gap paper in particular is only weeks old as of this writing and has no independent citations or commentary yet — treat as current best evidence, not settled consensus.
- Ramp's case (Finding 6) rests primarily on a single company engineering blog; the product's existence, timeline, and headline cost-savings figure are independently corroborated (Hacker News, a third-party tech article), but the specific Thompson-Sampling algorithm choice itself is not independently verified beyond Ramp's own account.
- No claim in this research pass covered Martian, NotDiamond, or Unify's actual internal routing mechanisms — this report cannot speak to whether those commercial routers are classifier-based, bandit-based, or something else; that remains an open gap.
- A claim asserting that FrugalGPT/HybridLLM/OpenRouter-style routing decisions are driven by "learned category heuristics rather than genuine complexity assessment" across *all* tested routers was checked and refuted (0-3) as a blanket statement — the category-based mis-routing finding (Finding 2) is specific to the BERT-based/RouteLLM-style architecture in one paper, not a universal property of every router type.

## Recommendation for Kelvran

| Capability | Status | Trigger to revisit |
|---|---|---|
| RouteLLM-style classifier/embedding router (matrix factorization, BERT, causal-LLM, similarity ranking) | **not_yet** | Labeled preference/complexity dataset at real volume — likely sourced from Kelvran's own evals/judge-panel pipeline once it has enough real traffic and judged outcomes |
| FrugalGPT-style scoring-function cascade | **not_yet** | Same labeled-data prerequisite as above for the acceptance threshold; Kelvran's existing error-classified fallback chain already covers hard-failure escalation with zero data cost |
| PILOT/OrcaRouter-style academic bandit routers (as shipped research artifacts) | **not_yet** | Would need to move from offline-simulation-validated to a real online deployment first — no shortcut here, this is upstream research risk, not a Kelvran-specific gap |
| Thompson-Sampling / simple contextual-bandit re-weighting layer over the existing WRR candidate pool, using `cost_tier.go` + `samplewindow.go` as reward signal (Ramp-style) | **build_now-adjacent** (needs a design pass, not new data) | None to *start* prototyping; needs a traffic-volume check before trusting bandit convergence at Kelvran's current scale — same floor-gated concern already named for router-health work |
| "Router must never be architecturally more expensive than the models it routes between" as a design constraint for any future work in this space | **build_now** (as a principle) | None — costs nothing to adopt now |

The one concretely low-risk next step is prototyping a bandit-style re-weighting layer on top of the existing WRR pool, using signals Kelvran already collects (cost tier, sample-window success/latency) as the reward — architecturally the same shape as Ramp's shipped production router, and requiring no labeled dataset or ML training pipeline. Everything classifier/preference-based (RouteLLM, FrugalGPT's scoring function, and the broader "quality-cost frontier" pattern) remains not-yet-ready for Kelvran specifically, and Finding 5's benchmark evidence suggests it may not be a reliably-beneficial pattern in general yet, independent of Kelvran's own data gap.

## Open Questions

- Does Kelvran have enough real per-deployment request volume today for a Thompson-Sampling arm-selection layer to converge meaningfully, or does bandit exploration need the same traffic-volume floor already named as a blocker for router-health/circuit-breaking work?
- What is Ramp Router's (router.com) actual production mechanism today, now that it has generalized from an internal tool into a multi-tenant commercial product — is it still pure Thompson Sampling, or has it since absorbed classifier/embedding signals?
- Given LLMRouterBench's finding that "model-recall failure" (not cost/quality miscalibration) is the dominant unrealized-value driver industry-wide, would Kelvran gain more from investing in better model-capability tracking/labeling per deployment than from any routing-algorithm sophistication?
- Could Kelvran's own evals/judge-panel pipeline eventually double as the labeled preference dataset a RouteLLM-style router would need, and if so, what coverage/volume threshold would make that a reasonable bet versus the bandit-only path?
