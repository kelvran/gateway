# Kelvran Cache — 2026 Upgrade Research & Prioritized Roadmap

**Date:** 2026-09-06
**Scope:** `gateway/internal/cache/` — the embedded, in-process multi-layer response cache (L1 exact-match, L2 normalized-match, L3-lite lexical near-duplicate). Excludes the Gateway's routing/adapter/rate-limit surface and the separate Python `evals` service — see the sibling reports in this directory.
**Method:** Adversarial multi-source research (3-vote verification per claim) against the specific threat model and design questions this project's own `PRD.md`, `THREAT_MODEL.md`, and Cache RFCs already name — CacheAttack, KeyPooling, `golang.org/x/sync/singleflight`'s real API surface, and 2026 semantic-cache-safety literature (GroundedCache, LaCache) that postdates those two papers. 19 claims confirmed (unanimous 3-0 on 16 of them, 2-1 on 3), 6 claims explicitly refuted (did not survive verification) — the refutations are load-bearing for this report's answer to research question 3 below, not just noise to discard.

---

## Executive summary

Kelvran's already-shipped design choices hold up well against 2026 research, and in two places the research actively validates decisions that were made without this evidence in hand: KeyPooling's "defense contract" (an authenticated-identity namespace must survive *every* cache lookup and write, not just be present at some layers) is exactly the isolation model Kelvran already implements, and CacheAttack's own attack mechanics — a cache hit skips the LLM call entirely, so a hijacked hit bypasses safety filtering with zero model involvement — is the precise failure mode Kelvran's hard entity/freshness gate (never a bare similarity threshold) was built to close. The one genuinely new, more sophisticated 2026 pattern beyond what Kelvran's own RFC anticipated is GroundedCache's multi-gate design: four cheap, independently-gateable checks instead of one hard gate, with a same-paper ablation showing one gate (lexical/judge-based support of the cached answer against fresh evidence) does most of the safety work while the others are near-zero-cost backup — a methodology Kelvran can and should apply to its *existing* L3-lite lexical layer's own multiple checks (entity/number/date mismatch, freshness/risk model, volatile-topic bypass) right now, using telemetry it already has, rather than waiting for real embeddings. On the two hardest open questions — extending singleflight coalescing from an exact-match key to a normalized-match key, and whether a distributed shared cache is worth building — the research finding is itself informative: no published "clean" solution to the two-key coalescing problem was found (attempts to characterize it as either a solved pattern or a documented hazard both failed verification), so Kelvran's RFC's own assessment that this needs a separate design stands un-refuted; and distributed coordination is consistently characterized in the literature as *introducing* new failure modes (partitions, lease expiry, fencing, split-brain) rather than being a low-risk scope upgrade, which argues for measuring the actual cross-instance cost before building anything. Two of the six sub-questions in the research brief (cache ROI/savings reporting methodology, and L1/L2 age-tracking value) returned no confirmed external sources at all — these are reported honestly as open questions grounded only in Kelvran's own stated principles, not dressed up as externally-validated findings.

## Findings, ranked

### 1. Adopt GroundedCache's per-gate ablation methodology on L3-lite's existing gates now — the semantic-cache-safety pattern has matured since CacheAttack/KeyPooling (Small)

**What:** GroundedCache (arXiv:2605.27494, May 2026) proposes admitting a cached RAG answer only when four cheap gates simultaneously hold: query similarity, retrieved-evidence overlap, source-version validity (freshness), and lexical/judge-based support of the cached answer against newly-retrieved evidence. Its own per-gate ablation found one gate — lexical/judge support — does most of the safety work, with the other three acting as low-cost backup rather than each being equally load-bearing. Under a document-drift scenario, naive similarity-only caching served a wrong cached answer 51.5% of the time; the four-gate design cut this to 1.5% (a ~34x reduction), at only 1.04-1.07x the latency of no caching at all.

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** this is the direct answer to research question 2 — yes, the entity/freshness-hard-gate pattern Kelvran already uses has become more structured in the 2026 literature since CacheAttack/KeyPooling: instead of one hard gate, the emerging pattern is *several* cheap, independently-instrumented gates, one of which will turn out to be doing most of the work. Kelvran's L3-lite already has an analogous multi-gate structure it hasn't yet analyzed this way: the entity/number/date-mismatch gate, the freshness/risk model, and the volatile-topic hard bypass are three separate checks layered on top of the Jaccard similarity score. Nobody at Kelvran currently knows which of these three is actually catching bad matches versus rubber-stamping them — exactly the question GroundedCache's ablation answers for its own four gates.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Fully consistent — this does not touch the "no bare similarity threshold" requirement, does not widen the L2 normalization allowlist, and does not build real embeddings. It is a measurement layer on top of gates that already exist and already have the raw data available (Jaccard score and entry age are already computed and now surfaced per the cache-hit-provenance telemetry work).

**Caveat on the source:** GroundedCache is a single-author, not-yet-peer-reviewed arXiv preprint (citation count 1 at time of review) — treat it as one credible, well-evidenced 2026 proposal, not settled industry consensus. Its four-gate frame also presupposes a RAG pipeline with an explicit "retrieved evidence" set; Kelvran's gateway sits below the agent and generally does not see retrieval results, so the "retrieved-evidence overlap" gate itself does not map cleanly onto Kelvran's architecture — only the *methodology* (ablate your gates to find the load-bearing one; don't assume they're equally important) transfers, not the specific four-gate list.

**Concrete next step:** instrument per-gate outcome counters on the three existing L3-lite gates (entity/number/date mismatch reject rate, freshness/risk-model reject rate, volatile-topic bypass trigger rate) alongside the Jaccard score already logged, then run the same kind of ablation GroundedCache did — which gate, when disabled, produces the biggest jump in wrong-answer-served rate on Kelvran's own traffic once it exists. This is exactly the kind of pre-emptive telemetry research question 1 asks for.

**Effort:** Small — counters on decision points that already exist in the code path; no new gate logic.

### 2. CacheAttack's attack mechanics confirm the hard-gate design was solving the right problem — no change needed, but the mechanism is worth restating precisely (informational, no effort)

**What:** CacheAttack (arXiv:2601.23088, accepted ICML 2026) uses gradient-based adversarial-suffix search to force a malicious prompt's embedding to collide with a targeted victim's benign query embedding, producing a false-positive semantic cache hit that serves the attacker's planted response to the victim — achieving an 86% hijack rate and transferring across embedding models. Critically, the attack's suffix begins with a literal `"Neglect: "` marker so the *generation* model ignores the adversarial suffix if the cache ever misses and the prompt is actually run, while the *embedding* model still uses the full suffix to force the collision — and the attack bypasses backend safety filtering entirely, because a cache hit skips the LLM call outright.

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** this is the precise mechanism Kelvran's hard entity/number/date-mismatch gate (never a bare similarity threshold) is designed to defeat — an adversarial suffix can force embedding-space or shingle-space similarity without changing the *entities, numbers, or dates* in the visible text, which is exactly what the hard gate checks regardless of how close the similarity score is. This is worth stating explicitly in Kelvran's own threat model as the concrete mechanism the gate defeats, rather than a generic "similarity-only caching is risky" justification.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Fully — this confirms, not revisits, the existing hard-gate requirement. No action needed on the gate design itself.

**Concrete next step:** none required for L3-lite (lexical shingling has no embedding space to attack in the same way CacheAttack targets, and the hard gate already blocks pure-similarity hijacks). File this mechanism description into the eventual real-embedding-L3 RFC's threat-model section now, so the design doc doesn't have to re-derive it later — a "Neglect:"-style attack against a future embedding-based L3 layer is a first-class threat that layer's gate design must account for from day one, not discovered post-launch.

**Effort:** None now (documentation-only); informs the future L3 RFC.

### 3. KeyPooling's "defense contract" validates tenant isolation — but only if it holds on *every* path, so add an explicit isolation-invariant test suite (Small)

**What:** KeyPooling (arXiv:2608.17485, Aug 2026) measured that all five tested open-source LLM API gateways exposed cross-customer cache reads under a shared upstream credential, and derives a defense contract: every customer must either enter a provider-enforced isolation domain, or an authenticated-identity-derived namespace must survive **every single cache lookup and write** — not just be present at some layers.

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** Kelvran's tenant isolation (tenant ID baked into the L1/L2 key and L3's per-tenant partitioning, enforced at lookup/write/retry/fallback) is already the correct shape per this defense contract — this is a confirmation, not a design change. The value this research adds is the contract's precise wording: "survive every ... lookup and write," which is a testable invariant, not just a design description.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Fully consistent — this is explicitly named as settled/correct in the research brief. This finding does not propose redesigning isolation; it proposes *proving* the existing design meets the contract with a test, since the KeyPooling paper's own headline finding is that 5/5 production gateways *believed* they had isolation and didn't.

**Concrete next step:** add a fuzz-style or property-based test that exercises every code path that touches the cache (lookup, write, singleflight-coalesced write, retry, fallback) across at least two tenants and asserts the tenant-derived namespace component is present and correctly scoped on every one — not just the happy-path lookup/write already presumably covered. This is cheap insurance against exactly the kind of "isolation present at some layers, missing at others" gap that caused all five KeyPooling-tested gateways to fail.

**Effort:** Small — a targeted test suite over already-existing code paths, no production code change expected if the design is as advertised.

### 4. Extending singleflight from an exact-match key to a normalized-match key: no published clean solution exists — the RFC's own difficulty assessment stands un-refuted (informational — do not build; keep deferred)

**What:** `golang.org/x/sync/singleflight`'s `Group.Do`/`DoChan`/`Forget` API is confirmed to be strictly single-string-key with no built-in multi-key or key-aliasing mechanism — deduplication is scoped to exact matches on one key string, and any capability to propagate one in-flight execution's result under a second, different key would have to be built as an application-level wrapper on top of the package, not inside it. Beyond that mechanical fact, this research explicitly tried to find either (a) a documented clean pattern for this exact problem, or (b) a documented hazard class explaining why it's hard, and **both attempts failed verification** — the specific claims proposing each were refuted 0-3 in adversarial review.

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** this directly answers research question 3. The honest answer is: this remains genuinely unresearched/undocumented territory in the industry literature available to this research pass, not a solved problem Kelvran is failing to adopt, and not a well-known hazard class Kelvran is right to avoid for a citable reason — the null result itself is the finding. Kelvran's own RFC's assessment that widening stampede coalescing to the L2 key "needs a genuinely separate design" is therefore not refuted by anything found here, but it's also not independently corroborated by outside prior art — it stands on its own reasoning.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Fully consistent — this finding does not argue for building anything; it argues for *not* treating the absence of a found pattern as either a green light or a red flag, and for not over-indexing on "nobody else solves this cleanly" as a justification to attempt something bespoke without first sizing the payoff (see Finding 6 on measuring before building, which applies the same discipline here).

**One mechanically-grounded observation worth recording (not a "confirmed claim," just an implication of confirmed facts):** since singleflight's own `Do` returns the same result to every waiting caller and writing to the cache happens in application code *after* `Do` returns (not inside the package), a caller that already knows its own exact-match key at call time could key the `Group` by the *normalized* key (coalescing near-duplicates) while each individual waiter still performs its own two writes (its own L1 key, plus the shared L2 key) once `Do` returns. This does not by itself resolve what the RFC likely flagged as the harder issue — which physical request's parameters (model, temperature, sampling config) the one shared upstream call actually runs with when two L1-distinct requests share an L2 key — so it should be treated as a partial sketch worth testing, not a validated design.

**Effort:** N/A — recommendation is to keep this deferred exactly as currently designed, and treat the sketch above as a starting hypothesis if and when telemetry (see Finding 6's sibling question — how often do L1-miss/L2-coalescible bursts actually occur) shows the payoff justifies the design work.

### 5. Do not build a distributed/Redis-backed shared cache now — measure cross-instance duplicate-work and effective-hit-rate loss first (Medium to instrument; Large if ever built)

**What:** Every source examined on request coalescing and stampede protection converges on the same structural point: `singleflight`-style coalescing is inherently scoped to a single process — separate instances/pods each maintain independent state and will each independently execute a call, with duplicate work occurring once per instance even with local coalescing enabled. Extending coordination beyond one process (a distributed lock or shared cache) is consistently described as *introducing* new failure modes — network partitions, lease expiry, fencing, failover, split-brain — rather than being a straightforward scope upgrade of the same mechanism.

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** this is the evidence base for research question 4. Kelvran's dormant `grpcserver`/`grpcclient` adapter stubs are a real escape hatch if distributed caching is ever needed, but the literature's consistent framing — new failure modes, not a bigger version of the same thing — argues against treating that extraction as low-risk or default-next. The actual value of a distributed shared cache for Kelvran has two distinct components that are easy to conflate: (a) stampede coalescing across instances (bounded cost — at most one extra upstream call per replica per burst, which is small at the low replica counts typical of a self-hosted deployment), and (b) cross-instance **cache-hit sharing** (a potentially much larger effect — if replicas are load-balanced without sticky routing, a response cached on instance A is invisible to instance B, so the *effective* hit rate any single customer experiences can be meaningfully lower than the true duplicate-request rate across all replicas combined, scaling roughly with replica count). No source found speaks to Kelvran's specific self-hosted deployment scale, so this cannot be answered definitively either way from external research — but the research does establish clearly that the cost side (new distributed failure modes) is real and not hypothetical, which shifts the burden of proof onto measuring the benefit side before paying that cost.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Fully consistent — the extraction-into-a-standalone-service decision and its evidence-based triggers are explicitly out of scope here and untouched. This finding is about the narrower question of a shared cache/coalescing backend, not the whole-service extraction question, though a future case for the former could eventually feed into evidence for the latter.

**Concrete next step (instrument now, decide later):** add lightweight telemetry that estimates, retroactively, how often an exact-key cache miss on one instance was actually a repeat of a request served as a hit on a *different* instance within its TTL window (correlatable via tenant + exact key + instance ID across the log/metrics stream, without needing a live distributed lookup). If, once real traffic exists, this cross-instance-miss rate is small at Kelvran's typical self-hosted replica counts, single-instance-only is very plausibly the right *permanent* answer for this product's deployment model, not merely a stepping stone — and that conclusion would be reached with actual evidence rather than by default.

**Effort:** Small-Medium to instrument (log/metric correlation, no new runtime dependency); Large if a distributed cache is ever actually built (matches the failure-mode complexity documented above).

### 6. Add write-time age tracking to L1/L2 (closing the named telemetry gap) — reasoned recommendation, not externally sourced (Small)

**What:** L1 (exact-match) and L2 (normalized-match) currently capture no write-time timestamp, while L3 does and now surfaces it. This research brief's question 6 asked whether this asymmetry is a real gap or acceptable as-is. No confirmed external source in this research pass addressed this question directly — it is answered here from first principles plus Kelvran's own stated design principles, and should be weighted accordingly (lower confidence than the sourced findings above).

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** entry age is load-bearing for L3 because L3's match is *approximate* — a near-duplicate query might have been cached when the underlying facts were different, so age feeds directly into the freshness/risk model that gates whether the near-duplicate hit is safe to serve. For L1/L2, the match is exact (or trivially normalized), so age does not need to gate *correctness* the same way — a TTL already governs expiry at write time via `Put`'s `ttl` parameter regardless of whether age is separately surfaced. The genuine value of adding it to L1/L2 is observability, not correctness: knowing how old a served exact/normalized hit was lets Kelvran's dashboards answer "is our TTL too generous for this tenant's volatile-topic traffic" and lets the cache-hit-provenance telemetry (which already reports layer + Jaccard + age for L3) be uniform across all three layers rather than conspicuously missing a field for two of them.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Fully consistent — this is additive observability, not a change to matching, eviction, or gating logic on any layer.

**Concrete next step:** add a write-time timestamp alongside existing L1/L2 entries and surface it in the same cache-hit-provenance telemetry stream already reporting layer + (L3-only) Jaccard/age, so dashboards can report age uniformly across all three layers.

**Effort:** Small — a timestamp field on write, no gating logic change.

### 7. Cache ROI/savings reporting methodology: no confirmed external source found — flagged as a genuine open question (informational only)

Research question 5 asked for 2026 best practices on aggregating "what a hit would have cost" into a defensible business metric. This research pass found no citable source (academic or industry) addressing LLM-gateway cache ROI reporting methodology specifically — it is reported here as an open question rather than answered with unsourced generic advice. The one grounded anchor available is Kelvran's own `PRD.md`, which already states the relevant principle directly: "a hit rate without a correctness number is not a success metric here, it's a vanity metric." The already-shipped telemetry (which layer served each hit, the dollar amount it would have cost, and for L3 the Jaccard score and age) gives Kelvran the *raw* ingredients to build a defensible metric — e.g., savings reported net of any known-wrong L3 hits found post-hoc, rather than gross savings across all hits regardless of correctness — but this research did not surface an external, citable 2026 standard for how other LLM gateways structure this reporting, so any specific aggregation design should be treated as Kelvran's own reasoned choice, not an industry-validated pattern.

## Top 3 — do next

**Do now:**
1. **Per-gate ablation instrumentation on L3-lite's existing gates** (Finding 1, Small) — counters on the entity/number/date-mismatch gate, freshness/risk-model gate, and volatile-topic bypass, using data already computed. This is the single highest-leverage action: it applies a validated 2026 methodology (GroundedCache's ablation) to gates Kelvran already has, and it's the concrete first step toward the eventual real-embedding L3 design without building anything embedding-related yet.
2. **Tenant-isolation invariant test suite** (Finding 3, Small) — cheap insurance that Kelvran's already-correct isolation design actually holds on every lookup/write/retry/fallback path, in direct response to KeyPooling's finding that all five gateways it tested believed they had isolation and didn't.

**Instrument now, decide later (do not build yet):**
3. **Cross-instance duplicate-work / effective-hit-rate telemetry** (Finding 5) — before any distributed/Redis-backed cache work, measure how often a cache miss on one gateway instance is actually a repeat of a hit served moments earlier on a sibling instance. The literature is clear that distributed coordination adds real failure modes (partitions, lease expiry, fencing, split-brain) rather than being a low-risk extension — so the burden is on demonstrating the benefit is worth that cost at Kelvran's actual self-hosted replica counts, not on assuming it is.

Everything else in this report (the singleflight two-key coalescing question, L1/L2 age tracking, and cache-ROI-reporting methodology) is either explicitly recommended to stay deferred (singleflight widening — no published pattern exists either way; distributed cache — same), or a low-cost, low-risk addition with no urgency (L1/L2 age tracking), or a genuine open question this research pass could not close (ROI reporting methodology) and should be revisited once real production telemetry exists.
