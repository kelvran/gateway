# Gateway Cost/FinOps & Multi-Tenant Economics — Round 4 Research (2026-09-11)

**Scope:** Kelvran gateway's cost/FinOps lens, compared against 2026 LLM-platform FinOps practice (AWS Cost Anomaly Detection, Portkey, Helicone, LiteLLM). Six research questions; findings below are the claims that survived 3-vote adversarial verification, merged and organized by question. "Build now" / "not yet" verdicts are given only where the surviving evidence actually supports one; two of the six sub-questions returned no surviving primary-source evidence this round and are flagged as open questions rather than answered from assumption.

**Already shipped (not re-litigated):** decimal-precision cost accounting against a static `price_table`; per-key `budget_usd` with rolling reset window + soft `budget_warn_percent`; Reserve/Reconcile TOCTOU-safe enforcement; cache-hit/coalesced-miss billing exclusion; cache-token pricing at a discount rate across Anthropic/Bedrock/OpenAI/best-effort-OpenAI-compat/Gemini-implicit-caching; `kelvran.cache.savings_usd` metric. **Already deferred (not re-litigated):** hierarchical org→team→user→key→session budget cascades (no team/org concept exists; zero demand signal).

---

## Executive Summary

AWS Cost Anomaly Detection is not a real "24-hour cold start" feature as some secondary sources imply — it requires a minimum of 10 days of historical spend data per new monitored entity plus an additional up-to-24-hour monitor-activation delay, and none of Kelvran's direct competitors (Helicone, Portkey) implement true spend-rate/baseline anomaly detection at all — both ship only static, manually-tuned percentage/dollar thresholds over fixed time windows. On cost allocation, Helicone has a real, dedicated, reserved-header session primitive (`Helicone-Session-Id`) that Helicone's own docs tie directly to per-interaction cost figures, while Portkey's equivalent is looser (free-form metadata tags + Usage-Limit-Policy group-by, with `session_id` only a documented convention, not reserved) — Kelvran's existing `AgentRunIDFromContext` plumbing already clears the bar either competitor sets, making a cost-by-agent-run breakdown a low-effort, currently-missing primitive worth adding now. Price-table staleness is a proven real risk, not a theoretical one: LiteLLM shipped an incorrect Bedrock Claude 4.6 surcharge for ~6 weeks, caught by an external community PR rather than any automated check — no vendor was found with live automated price-staleness detection, meaning a periodic drift-check job would be a genuine (if bespoke) differentiator. Multi-currency pricing and cost-aware routing tie-breaking returned no surviving primary-source evidence this round and remain open rather than resolved.

## Findings

### Finding 1 — Cost anomaly detection: real gap, but not buildable-now as a true baseline detector; no competitor has solved it either
**Confidence: high** (10 sources, primarily 3-0 primary-source votes; corroborated by 5 independently refuted counter-claims)

AWS Cost Anomaly Detection — the most sophisticated anomaly system checked — requires **at least 10 days of historical usage data** before it can detect anomalies for a new monitored entity (a new service subscription, or by extension any newly-onboarded cost dimension), on top of a separate **up-to-24-hour monitor-activation delay** that applies once an entity already has spend history. These are two distinct, additive constraints per AWS's own current documentation (`docs.aws.amazon.com/cost-management/.../manage-ad.html` and the AWS Cost Anomaly Detection FAQ), not alternate readings of the same fact — five separate claims asserting a pure "signal within 24 hours, no historical-data prerequisite" reading were each independently refuted 0-3 against the same and adjacent primary sources. Onboarding itself is a simple 3-step, AWS-managed-monitor flow (no manual threshold definition) built on a multi-layered ML model that learns historic spend patterns automatically — the console "threshold" only gates *notifications*, not detection itself, which runs continuously regardless of alert threshold.

Neither Helicone nor Portkey — Kelvran's closer competitive set — implements anything resembling this. Helicone's cost alerting is a static dollar-amount-over-fixed-window threshold (e.g., "$1000 over last 24 hours"), optionally broken down by User/Model/Provider/custom-property *grouping* — but grouping is alert-scoping, not a rolling-baseline comparison, and Helicone's own cost-tracking cookbook explicitly recommends manual graduated percentage tripwires (50/80/95% of budget) as its anomaly-adjacent practice. Portkey's budget/quota alerting is likewise a single static percentage-of-budget threshold (documented example: 80%), with no spend-velocity or historical-baseline mechanism anywhere in its docs.

**Verdict: not yet.** A genuine per-key spend-rate-vs-own-trailing-baseline detector faces the same cold-start problem AWS's own system has, and Kelvran has strictly less historical signal to calibrate against than AWS does (AWS calibrates against an entire account's service-level spend history; Kelvran would calibrate per virtual key, a narrower and noisier signal). **Named trigger:** once individual virtual keys have accumulated on the order of AWS's own minimum (~10+ days of stable spend history) in production, a simple trailing-average/rate-of-change comparison becomes statistically meaningful rather than noise; before that point, shipping it would just be a differently-labeled static threshold wearing an "anomaly detection" name. This is also not a competitive-parity gap — no checked vendor (AWS's own product excepted, and it solves a different problem at a different granularity) has shipped this for per-key LLM spend, so there is no market pressure forcing an early build.

### Finding 2 — Cost allocation/chargeback: real, low-effort gap Kelvran can close now via existing telemetry
**Confidence: medium-high** (7 sources; mostly 3-0, two Portkey-specific sub-claims at 2-1)

Helicone's real granularity ceiling below a single API key is session-based: `Helicone-Session-Id` / `Helicone-Session-Name` (and `-Path`) are dedicated, reserved headers that group related requests, and Helicone's cost-tracking documentation explicitly ties this grouping to per-interaction cost figures (its own cited example: "a support chat costs $0.12 on average with 5 API calls"). The dedicated Sessions feature page itself frames the mechanism only as request-tracing/debugging and contains no cost math — the cost-attribution framing lives specifically in the cost-tracking cookbook — but the underlying header mechanism is the same, reserved, first-class primitive either way.

Portkey's equivalent is a step looser: no reserved session field exists in its metadata schema (only `_user` is special/reserved, auto-copied from OpenAI's `user` field and used for dashboard analytics); `session_id` is listed only as a suggested, non-enforced naming convention alongside `environment`, `feature`, `version`, and `request_id`. Portkey's real granularity primitive is request-level metadata tagging (strings, ≤128 chars/value, no documented cap on key count per request) combined with Usage Limit Policies' condition + "group-by" logic — tag every request with an identifier, and that identifier gets its own independent budget counter. Portkey explicitly documents this as a cost-attribution/chargeback use case ("attribute AI costs to teams, features, or products"), and the granularity can go below user-level (e.g., per-prompt-type budgets). Both vendors' ceilings are therefore some flavor of "tag a request with a sub-identity, then group/query cost by that tag" — Helicone via a reserved header, Portkey via free-form metadata.

**Verdict: build now.** Kelvran's own `AgentRunIDFromContext` telemetry plumbing already threads an equivalent sub-identity through the request lifecycle — the missing piece is not instrumentation but an aggregation/query surface (a cost-by-`agent_run_id` breakdown within a virtual key), which is cheaper to add than either competitor's mechanism was to build from scratch, since the identifier already exists in telemetry rather than needing new header/metadata plumbing. **Named trigger:** none needed beyond scheduling the work — the primitive competitors ship at this granularity is already matched by Kelvran's existing instrumentation; this is a reporting/query feature, not a new capability. (Caveat: two LiteLLM-specific claims about its own session/metadata chargeback ceiling did not survive adversarial verification at the 3-vote bar, so LiteLLM's own granularity ceiling remains unconfirmed and is excluded from this comparison — the finding rests on Helicone + Portkey only.)

### Finding 3 — Price-table staleness: proven real risk; no vendor has automated it, so building one is a bespoke differentiator
**Confidence: high** (1 source, but a directly-verified primary GitHub PR cross-checked against live current upstream state and a maintainer's independent confirmation)

LiteLLM's static, manually-maintained Bedrock price-table entries for Claude Opus/Sonnet 4.6 incorrectly carried a 2x above-200k-token surcharge — a real charge AWS Bedrock does not apply — for roughly six weeks (introduced 2026-02-06, fixed 2026-03-23) before an external, one-time community contributor caught and fixed it via PR, not any LiteLLM-internal tooling. A maintainer independently confirmed the same 2x-surcharge error existed on the Anthropic-direct and Vertex AI price-table entries too, and none of these providers should apply such a surcharge for 4.6-series models — i.e., this was not an isolated typo but a systemic staleness failure mode across multiple provider entries in a widely-used, community-maintained pricing file, caught entirely by luck of an external reviewer rather than by process.

**Verdict: build now** (as a bounded ops safeguard, not a new runtime dependency). The risk is empirically proven at a major, well-resourced project with a much larger contributor base than Kelvran's, and the fix is a "boil the lake"-scale problem: a periodic (e.g., weekly, CI-scheduled) job that diffs `price_table` entries against each provider's own live pricing surface (a documentation page, a pricing API where one exists, or at minimum a stored pricing checksum reviewed against provider changelogs) and alerts on drift. **Named trigger:** none needed beyond deciding to schedule the work — the failure mode is already demonstrated, not merely hypothetical. **Caveat:** no vendor (LiteLLM, Helicone, Portkey) was found to have a live, automated price-staleness-detection feature to copy the design from — this would be a genuinely novel internal tool with no reference implementation, so scope it conservatively (diff + alert, not auto-correct) to avoid over-building.

### Finding 4 — Multi-currency/regional pricing: no evidence gathered, premature
**Confidence: n/a — open question, not a verified finding**

No claim in this research round's confirmed or refuted sets addresses multi-currency or regional pricing at all — this sub-question was not pursued to a verifiable primary source this pass. Given Kelvran has no team/org/multi-tenant billing concept yet (hierarchical budgets are explicitly deferred with zero demand signal per prior research), multi-currency sits even further down the same deferred stack. **Not yet**, by YAGNI default rather than by disconfirming evidence — **named trigger:** a genuine non-USD billing or multi-region customer requirement surfacing, which no research pass to date has found signal for.

### Finding 5 — Cost-aware routing tie-breaker: no evidence gathered this round
**Confidence: n/a — open question, not a verified finding**

No surviving claim addresses whether a cost-aware routing tie-breaker (prefer the cheaper of two equally-healthy deployments serving the same canonical model) is real and buildable-now, distinct from the already-deferred autonomous cost-optimization routing. This sub-question needs its own dedicated research pass before a verdict can be given — asserting one here would not be backed by verified evidence.

## Caveats

- Two LiteLLM-specific claims (its own session/agent-run cost-attribution ceiling, and whether its pricing file has any staleness-detection mechanism) were each refuted at a 1-2 vote split and are excluded from Finding 2 and Finding 3 respectively. LiteLLM's *price-table bug itself* (Finding 3) is independently and separately confirmed at 3-0 via a different, directly-verified GitHub PR — that part of the finding stands regardless.
- A claim that OpenRouter's models page functions as a live, real-time cross-provider pricing reference was refuted (1-2) — do not treat OpenRouter's listing page as a validated live-price oracle for a future staleness-detection design.
- The AWS Cost Anomaly Detection findings are about AWS's own account/service-level product, not a per-LLM-API-key product — the 10-day/24-hour figures are the closest available real-world precedent for "how much history does a baseline-anomaly system need," not a direct benchmark of what a per-virtual-key system would require (which could plausibly need more, given noisier/lower-volume signal per key).
- Findings 4 and 5 are explicitly unresolved by this research round, not "researched and found to be non-issues" — they should be treated as backlog items for a future dedicated pass, not closed out.

## Open Questions

1. What is LiteLLM's actual, verifiable sub-key cost-attribution granularity ceiling (session/agent-run vs. plain metadata bag)? The two claims addressing this failed adversarial verification and need a cleaner primary-source pass.
2. Is there any vendor, in or outside the LLM-gateway space, with a genuinely automated (not community-PR-dependent) price-table staleness detector Kelvran could take design cues from, or is a bespoke diff-and-alert job truly novel here?
3. Once Finding 1's named trigger is met (per-key spend history reaches AWS's own ~10-day calibration floor), what statistical method (simple trailing-average ratio vs. something more AWS-ML-like) is appropriate given LLM per-key spend is likely burstier/lower-volume than AWS's account-level service spend?
4. Multi-currency pricing and cost-aware routing tie-breaking (research questions 4 and 5) need their own dedicated research passes — no primary-source evidence was gathered on either this round.
