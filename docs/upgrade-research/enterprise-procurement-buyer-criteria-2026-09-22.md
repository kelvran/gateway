## Executive Summary

The general-compliance research already correctly concluded that Kelvran-the-OSS-project pursuing SOC 2/ISO 42001 certification is a category error (no operating entity to certify) and that most compliance obligations land on the *operator*, not the project. This report asks a narrower, genuinely different question: not "is Kelvran compliant" but "does Kelvran's current posture let it clear the specific gates a real enterprise buyer's procurement/security-review process applies to any AI infrastructure vendor in 2026" — and the answer is more concrete and more actionable than the prior report's framing suggests. Every one of the five competitors named in Kelvran's own `PRD.md`/`README.md` comparison table (LiteLLM, Portkey, TensorZero, Bifrost, Kong AI Gateway) is a self-hosted-capable, open-source-core product with a **commercial legal entity standing behind it** that sells an Enterprise tier gating exactly the controls a 2026 enterprise buyer's checklist asks for: SSO/SCIM, audit logs with export, RBAC, a signed BAA, a SOC 2 Type II report *for the vendor's own organization*, and a support SLA. Kelvran has zero such entity — `SUPPORT.md` states plainly "Commercial/Enterprise Support: Not offered" and "best-effort, no SLA, at the current solo-maintainer stage." This is not a compliance gap in the sense the prior report addressed (a missing certification Kelvran structurally cannot obtain) — it is a **procurement-process gap**: there is no legal party to send a vendor-risk questionnaire to, sign an MSA/DPA/BAA with, or hold to an SLA, which is the single most load-bearing prerequisite every 2026 enterprise AI-vendor questionnaire surveyed (five independently-published templates, all converging) assumes exists before any of the AI-specific questions even get asked. The good news, also grounded in real precedent (Philterd's and Twingate's own published "why we don't sign a BAA" reasoning): Kelvran's self-hosted-only architecture means it never needs a HIPAA BAA or its own SOC 2 in the first place, for structurally the same reason those two real self-hosted vendors give — no persistent access to the customer's data, no business-associate relationship to govern. The buyer-facing risk is not that this is unresolvable — it's that, unlike Philterd/Twingate, Kelvran has no equivalent one-page answer ready when a procurement checklist asks the question reflexively (every survey template checked has that exact question). FedRAMP, separately, structurally does not apply to Kelvran at all under fedramp.gov's own 2026 scope rules — it gates *shared-responsibility cloud service offerings*, and a self-hosted, single-organization-operated deployment is an explicit, named exclusion — so unlike Kong (which must reconcile a SaaS control-plane's own FedRAMP posture with its self-hosted data-plane story), Kelvran has no FedRAMP story to build at all, which is worth stating affirmatively rather than leaving as an unaddressed silence a buyer's checklist will otherwise flag as "unknown/unanswered." The most concrete, previously-undocumented gap this pass surfaced through direct engagement with a real EU AI Act Article 26 deployer checklist: an enterprise buyer whose downstream use of Kelvran is itself classified high-risk under Annex III must "retrieve the automatic logs and reconcile system, policy, decision, and effect identifiers" for at least six months — and Kelvran's structured JSON audit log (shipped 2026-09-11) has, per `DECISIONS.md`'s own already-disclosed gap, "no query mechanism beyond scanning raw lines." That gap was previously framed only as an ops nicety; grounded against a real deployer checklist, it is a literal buyer-facing blocker for exactly the regulated-industry deals Kelvran's own PRD's "governance/accountability" differentiation thesis targets.

## Findings, ranked

### 1. Kelvran has no legal entity to be a "vendor" in the procurement sense — this is the single most load-bearing, previously-undocumented gate, distinct from the SOC 2 category-error finding (Large)

**What:** Every real enterprise vendor-risk-management (TPRM) process surveyed treats "there is a legal entity to contract with" as the load-bearing prerequisite that comes *before* any AI-specific security question gets asked — you cannot sign an MSA, DPA, BAA, or SLA with a solo-maintainer OSS repository. `SUPPORT.md` states this plainly for Kelvran today: "Commercial/Enterprise Support: Not offered... best-effort, no SLA, at the current solo-maintainer stage," and "No dedicated community channel exists yet."

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** the prior compliance research's Finding 2 ("SOC 2 is a category error — no operating entity exists") correctly diagnosed the *certification* half of this problem but did not connect it to the *procurement-process* half, which is broader and bites earlier. A real enterprise security/procurement reviewer does not stop at "no SOC 2 report" — the standard workflow (per Kusari's own "Third-Party Risk in Open Source" analysis and the New Stack's "Open Source Risk in the Procurement Process" piece, both independently describing the same pattern) is to route pure community OSS with no vendor relationship through an *internal* build/adopt review (in-house security team audits the code directly, no vendor contract at all) rather than the standard vendor-risk workflow — which is a real, viable path for Kelvran, but a fundamentally different sales motion than any competitor's own docs assume, and one Kelvran's own docs don't currently name or support (no security-review one-pager, no "how to adopt Kelvran without a vendor contract" guide). Separately, and more concretely: every one of the five competitors named in Kelvran's own comparison table backs its self-hosted-capable OSS core with a real company selling an Enterprise license precisely to answer this gate — LiteLLM Inc. ("SOC 2 Type 2 and ISO 27001, SSO and audit logs — on a gateway that runs in your own infrastructure" — litellm.ai/enterprise), Portkey/PRISMA AIRS (SOC 2 Type 2, ISO 27001, custom BAAs, on-prem deployment — portkey.ai/for/enterprise), Kong Inc. (SOC 2 Type II confirmed dated 2026-06-21 per trust.konghq.com, covering "Kong Gateway Enterprise, Kong AI Gateway" by name), TensorZero Inc. ($7.3M VC-backed seed round, a named CEO/team, already deployed at "one of Europe's largest banks" per its own case study), and Bifrost/Maxim AI (legal entity H3 Labs Inc., SOC 2 Type II + ISO 27001 + HIPAA + GDPR, "trusted by multiple Fortune 500 companies across financial services, healthcare... and defense"). Kelvran alone, among every product it positions itself against, has no equivalent.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Directly engages with, but does not contradict, `PRD.md`'s Non-Goal ("Kelvran is not, at this stage, a managed/hosted product. Self-hosting is the only supported deployment model until there's a real signal otherwise") — self-hosted-only and vendor-entity-backed are not mutually exclusive (every competitor above proves this: all five ship a self-hosted core, all five still have a company). This finding does not recommend Kelvran become a hosted/SaaS product (still correctly out of scope); it observes that "self-hosted" and "no company at all" are two independent axes the project has conflated, and every real peer decouples them.

**2026 best practice grounding:** Kusari's TPRM-for-OSS analysis and the New Stack's procurement-process piece both independently describe the same two-track pattern (internal-review track for no-vendor OSS vs. standard vendor-risk track for anything with a commercial entity) [kusari.dev, thenewstack.io — confirmed 2-0, consistent independent framing, not a head-to-head 3-vote adversarial check]. The five competitor vendor pages (LiteLLM, Portkey, Kong, TensorZero, Bifrost/Maxim) are primary-source, directly read — each vendor's own claim about its own entity/certifications is taken as a vendor claim, not independently audited by this research pass, which is the appropriate confidence grade for a vendor's own marketing/docs page rather than a third-party attestation registry lookup [primary vendor sources — high confidence on "a named entity + published certification claim exists," not independently re-verified against the certifying body].

**Concrete next step:** this is explicitly **not** a recommendation to incorporate a company or start selling support contracts — that is a business decision several steps beyond what a research pass should decide. The cheap, `build_now`-shaped action is documentation-only: write a short "Adopting Kelvran without a vendor contract" section (in `SUPPORT.md` or a new `docs/operations/PROCUREMENT.md`) that explicitly names the internal-security-review path as the real, viable route for a solo-maintainer OSS project (per the Kusari/New Stack pattern above), rather than leaving procurement teams to independently discover that Kelvran doesn't fit their default vendor-risk workflow. ### 2. Kelvran structurally does not need a HIPAA BAA — real precedent exists (Philterd, Twingate) for exactly this self-hosted architecture, but Kelvran has no equivalent public-facing answer for the question every buyer checklist asks anyway (Small)

**What:** Every AI-vendor questionnaire template surveyed (Accuroai's 50-question template Q26, Areebi's 60-question template, Portkey's own enterprise page's "Custom BAAs" line) includes a reflexive "does the vendor offer a BAA?" question. HHS's own guidance and a real, directly-comparable precedent — Philterd, a self-hosted PII-redaction toolkit, and Twingate, a self-hosted zero-trust network product — both publish a dedicated page arguing they are not HIPAA business associates at all, because their software runs entirely inside the customer's own environment and never has persistent ("routine," not merely "transient") access to the customer's PHI, which HHS's own FAQ and Omnibus Rule commentary confirm is the exact line the "conduit exception" draws (a data-storage or data-processing provider is a business associate even without viewing the data; a pure-transmission/self-hosted conduit with no persistent access is not).

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** Kelvran's architecture — self-hosted inside the operator's own infrastructure, no separate Kelvran-controlled service ever receives, maintains, or transmits the operator's data on Kelvran's own systems — is structurally the *same* shape as Philterd's and Twingate's, and stronger than the FAQ's own worked example (a data-storage company is a BA "even if [it] does not actually view the information," because storage *is* persistent access; Kelvran's L1/L2/L3 cache persists data, but on the *operator's own* infrastructure, never on any system Kelvran-the-project controls — there is no separate entity with access at all, not merely an entity that chooses not to look). This means the correct, defensible answer to "does Kelvran sign a BAA" is "there is no business-associate relationship for Kelvran-the-software to govern" — the same conclusion the prior compliance research reached for SOC 2, now independently corroborated for HIPAA via two real vendors' own published reasoning rather than derived from first principles alone. The buyer-facing risk is narrower than "Kelvran can't clear HIPAA" (it already structurally can, for anyone who reads the architecture correctly) — it is that a procurement reviewer who reflexively checks the "BAA?" box and finds no page addressing it at all will likely score it as a **missing/unresolved answer**, not as "correctly not applicable," which is a worse outcome for a fast-moving deal than either a signed BAA or a clearly-reasoned non-applicability page.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Directly corroborates, with new external precedent, the prior compliance research's implicit BAA framing (never explicitly stated as a BAA question in that report, which focused on SOC 2/EU AI Act/GDPR-erasure/data-residency) — this is a genuinely new sub-question that report didn't ask, not a re-litigation.

**2026 best practice grounding:** HHS.gov's own FAQ on cloud-service-provider conduit status and its Business Associates guidance page state the persistent-vs-transient-access test directly [hhs.gov — confirmed, primary regulatory source, cross-checked against a second HHS.gov guidance page and Holland & Hart's law-firm summary — 3-0]. Philterd's own published reasoning ("Self-hosted software running entirely inside your own environment... cannot create a business-associate relationship with the software vendor") and Twingate's own published BAA-refusal reasoning (conduit exception, transient-not-persistent access) are both real, current vendor precedent for exactly this architectural shape [philterd.ai, twingate.com — confirmed 2-0, both primary vendor sources making structurally identical arguments independently].

**Concrete next step:** write a short, public "Why Kelvran doesn't need a BAA" explainer (mirroring Philterd's own structure: definition → the two architectural shapes → which one Kelvran is → the one caveat that changes the analysis) as a new `docs/operations/HIPAA-BAA.md` or a section in `SECURITY.md` alongside the existing "Compliance Evidence for Operators" section — cheap, and directly answers a question this research confirms every serious buyer checklist asks. Should explicitly note the one real caveat: this reasoning holds for Kelvran-the-software; it says nothing about the operator's own separate BAA obligations with each upstream LLM provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, Bedrock all offer their own BAAs directly to the covered entity) — those are unaffected and remain the operator's own responsibility.

**Effort:** Small — a single new doc, no code change, reuses reasoning and structure this research has already assembled.

### 3. FedRAMP structurally does not apply to Kelvran at all — a genuinely different, more favorable position than any of the competitors surveyed, worth stating affirmatively rather than leaving silent (Small)

**What:** fedramp.gov's own 2026 scope guidance defines FedRAMP's scope as cloud computing products/services (IaaS/PaaS/SaaS) that create, collect, process, store, or maintain federal information *on behalf of* a federal agency under a shared-responsibility model — and explicitly excludes "information systems that are only used for a single agency's operations, hosted on cloud infrastructure or platform, and are not offered as a shared service or do not operate with a shared responsibility model," giving the specific worked example of "virtual 'on-prem' solutions that are simply deployed on agency managed cloud infrastructure then configured, operated, and supported by the agency" as squarely out of scope.

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** Kelvran is exactly this excluded case — self-hosted software an agency deploys, configures, and operates entirely inside its own boundary, with no Kelvran-controlled shared service or control plane anywhere in the picture (unlike, per this research's own read of a comparative industry analysis, Kong AI Gateway's Konnect control plane, which is a real SaaS shared-responsibility surface Kong itself must reconcile against a "FedRAMP-aligned reference architecture" story, or Kong's own FIPS 140-3 cryptography package, which exists specifically because Kong's product touches federal cryptographic-module requirements Kelvran's pure self-hosted-Go-binary shape does not need a special package for). This means Kelvran's FedRAMP position is not "not yet achieved" (the framing every certification gap in this space defaults to) but "correctly, structurally out of scope" — a stronger and more favorable claim than any competitor surveyed can make for their own SaaS control-plane components, and one worth stating explicitly, because an unaddressed FedRAMP question on a federal buyer's checklist reads as "unknown," while an explicit, correctly-reasoned "not applicable, here's why" reads as "vetted."

**Consistent with settled decisions?** New — no prior Kelvran research pass has engaged with FedRAMP at all. Does not conflict with anything in `PRD.md`/`DECISIONS.md`.

**2026 best practice grounding:** fedramp.gov's own current (2026) scope page states the shared-responsibility/single-agency exclusion directly, with the "virtual on-prem" example matching Kelvran's architecture almost verbatim [fedramp.gov — confirmed, primary regulatory source, cross-checked against the FedRAMP CSP Authorization Playbook PDF's identical "private cloud... within federal facilities... not subject to the FedRAMP mandate" language — 2-0 primary-source agreement]. Kong's own FedRAMP positioning (a "FedRAMP-aligned reference architecture" for its self-hosted plane, distinct FIPS 140-3 packaging, and an independent industry comparison's framing of "the procurement path is 'extend the existing Kong ATO to include the AI Proxy plugin'") is drawn from one comparative secondary source (futureagi.com's blog) rather than Kong's own primary docs for the FedRAMP-specific claims — flagged as **lower confidence** than the fedramp.gov scope-rule claim itself, included here for contrast only, not asserted as independently verified.

**Concrete next step:** a short "FedRAMP applicability" note (in `SECURITY.md` or the same new procurement-facing doc as Finding 1/2) stating the exclusion and citing fedramp.gov's own scope rule directly — cheap, and pre-empts a federal buyer's checklist item that would otherwise sit unanswered.

**Effort:** Small — a documentation note, no code change.

### 4. The already-disclosed "no queryable audit log" gap is a literal EU AI Act Article 26 deployer-checklist blocker, not just an ops nicety — re-engaging a known gap with new, buyer-specific evidence (Medium)

**What:** `DECISIONS.md`'s own 2026-09-15 entry already discloses: "A queryable audit-log surface (the structured JSON audit log, shipped 2026-09-11, has no query mechanism beyond scanning raw lines)... named as real, plausible next targets but were not scoped into this round's own 5 phases." This research checked that disclosed gap against a real EU AI Act Article 26 deployer-obligations checklist (three independently-published versions — the EU's own AI Act Service Desk text, a professional compliance-toolkit PDF, and a "runtime checklist" — all converged on the same operational demand) and found it maps directly onto a named, checkable buyer requirement, not merely an internal-tooling convenience.

**Why it matters for Kelvran specifically:** Article 26(6) requires a deployer of a high-risk AI system to "keep the logs automatically generated by that high-risk AI system... for a period appropriate to the intended purpose... of at least six months." One of the three checklists read (`kla.digital`'s runtime checklist) operationalizes this into a concrete evidentiary test: "Retrieve the automatic logs and reconcile system, policy, decision, and effect identifiers" for "one representative case" — i.e., a deployer's own compliance sign-off explicitly requires demonstrating retrieval of a *specific* run's logs on demand, not merely proving logs exist somewhere. Separately, the independently-published 2026 AI-vendor-questionnaire surveys (DeepInspect's 27-question template Q18-Q20, Accuroai's 50-question template's "Audit, logging, evidence" domain) both ask the vendor-side version of the identical question ("What audit fields does the vendor record per AI decision?", "How long does the vendor retain the AI-decision audit?", "Can the customer export the AI-decision audit?"). Kelvran's own audit log is real and already correctly structured (per `DECISIONS.md`'s 2026-09-09/2026-09-15 entries: structured JSON, key-name-only, Merkle/HMAC concerns aside) — but "scanning raw lines" is not a defensible answer to either the deployer's own Article 26(6) retrieval-and-reconciliation test or a buyer's own procurement-questionnaire export question, for any operator whose downstream use of Kelvran is itself classified high-risk (credit decisions, employment screening, and similarly Annex-III-shaped agent use cases are exactly the kind of workload Kelvran's own `agent_run_id`-level cost/governance-accountability thesis targets). This is a genuinely new angle on an old, already-disclosed gap — not a rediscovery of it, and not a contradiction of the prior round's decision to defer it, since that decision was made without checking it against a real deployer checklist.

**Consistent with settled decisions?** Re-engages, with new evidence, `DECISIONS.md`'s 2026-09-15 decision to name-but-not-scope this gap. Does not argue the prior deferral was wrong at the time — it was reasonable to defer an "ops nicety" with no demand signal. This finding supplies the demand signal the prior entry lacked: a concrete, checkable regulatory-checklist line item and two independent buyer-questionnaire templates both asking for exactly this capability, which changes the trigger condition from "no demand signal" to "a real, named, checkable one now exists in the regulatory-compliance literature," even absent an actual customer having asked yet.

**2026 best practice grounding:** the EU AI Act's own Article 26(6) text (ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu, the Commission's own service desk) is the primary source [confirmed, official EU source]. Two independently-produced professional compliance checklists (regulatoryai.eu's "Deployer Obligations Checklist," kla.digital's "Article 26 deployer runtime checklist," and a Latham & Watkins law-firm summary PDF) all independently operationalize the same retrieval/reconciliation requirement, though these are third-party interpretive artifacts, not binding law themselves [confirmed 3-0 on the underlying Article 26(6) text; the specific "retrieve and reconcile a representative case" operational framing is drawn from one of the three (kla.digital) and is a reasonable, but not independently-legislated, operationalization — flagged as such rather than asserted as itself a legal requirement]. The buyer-questionnaire-side corroboration (DeepInspect, Accuroai) is drawn from two independently-published 2026 vendor-questionnaire templates converging on the identical audit-export ask [confirmed 2-0].

**Concrete next step:** the smallest defensible improvement is not a full query engine — it's a `kelvran audit query --trace-id <id>` (or `--time-range`) CLI subcommand or admin-API endpoint that filters the existing structured JSON log by a small set of fields (trace_id, virtual_key_id, time range) without building a database. This directly answers both the Article 26(6) retrieval test and the buyer-questionnaire export question with the cheapest possible mechanism, reusing the log format that already exists rather than redesigning it.

**Effort:** Medium — a filtering CLI/endpoint over an existing, already-structured log format; no new storage engine, no schema change.
