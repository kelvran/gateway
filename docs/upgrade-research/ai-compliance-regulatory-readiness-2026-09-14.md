## Findings

1. **EU AI Act — Kelvran's gateway-layer routing/caching does not, by design, constitute a regulated "AI system," and merely routing traffic to third-party GPAI models does not make Kelvran a "provider" of those models.** Kelvran's routing (WRR/health-based, deterministic — `router/wrr.go`, `health.go`, `samplewindow.go`) and its L3 cache (pure lexical Jaccard/MinHash shingling, no ML) fall within the Commission's own "basic data processing"/"rules defined solely by natural persons" exclusion from the Article 3(1) "AI system" definition (guidelines paras 26/40/46). Independently, merely integrating/routing to a third-party GPAI model — absent a modification whose training compute exceeds ~1/3 of the original model's training compute — does not make the integrator a "provider" of that model (GPAI scope guidelines paras 61-63); Kelvran does no training/fine-tuning, nowhere near that threshold. Article 25(1)'s three provider-reclassification triggers (rebranding, substantial modification, repurposing to high-risk) describe nothing Kelvran's routing/caching does. **One live residual conditional, not fully closed:** per the EU AI Office's own Q&A, a downstream entity must still comply with AI-system obligations if its own offering qualifies as (or incorporates) an in-scope AI system, regardless of GPAI-model-provider status — this doesn't currently bite since nothing shipped exercises "inference" in the Act's sense, but it would need re-asking if the deferred real-embedding L3 semantic cache (already flagged as future work, gated on production telemetry) ever ships, since embedding-similarity scoring sits closer to "inference" than lexical shingling. **`build_now`**: a short (5-10 line) "EU AI Act scoping rationale" note in `THREAT_MODEL.md` or a new compliance doc, citing the basic-data-processing exclusion and the GPAI training-compute threshold. **`not_yet`**: any deeper program (Art. 6 classification review, conformity assessment, EU representative) — nothing today triggers it. *(High confidence, though the relied-on guidance is explicitly non-binding Commission interpretation, not CJEU precedent.)*

2. **SOC 2 — not something Kelvran-the-OSS-project can itself be; the real, relevant target is an operator's own audit of their deployment, and Kelvran's existing artifacts already double as much of that evidentiary backbone.** SOC 2 is an attestation an operating organization produces via an independent CPA audit of its own controls over time — it is not a property that attaches to open-source software itself; there is no "entity" to audit in a bare codebase. Separately, when an operator builds on infrastructure they don't fully control, the provider's own SOC 2 report only covers the provider's environment — the operator must still produce separate evidence of the controls *they* run, directly analogous to an operator self-hosting Kelvran needing to document their own controls plus track each upstream LLM provider's own certification posture. Applied to Kelvran: (a) Kelvran itself pursuing "SOC 2 compliance" is a category error — no Kelvran-the-vendor entity exists; (b) an operator wanting their own SOC 2 to cover a Kelvran deployment would draw on `THREAT_MODEL.md`'s STRIDE tables, `SECURITY.md`'s severity taxonomy/disclosure process, `SECURITY-INSIGHTS.yml`, and the cosign/SBOM/SLSA-provenance artifacts already shipped. Several more specific candidate mappings (exact Trust Services Criteria CC9.2/CC3.2, the "carve-out method" as default vendor treatment, a concrete CUEC customer-evidence artifact list) were checked and did **not** survive adversarial verification (refuted 0-3 / 1-2) — this finding deliberately stops short of asserting those specifics. **`build_now`**: a short operator-facing doc note (`SECURITY.md` or a new `docs/operations/OPERATOR-COMPLIANCE.md`) pointing at which existing Kelvran artifacts an operator's own auditor would want to see. **`not_yet`**: Kelvran itself obtaining a SOC 2 report — not applicable absent an operating entity; would only become relevant if Kelvran became a hosted/SaaS offering, which `PRD.md` explicitly rules out as a non-goal. *(Medium confidence — 2-1/3-0 on the two surviving claims.)*

3. **Data residency / sub-processor DPA disclosure — `PROVIDERS.md` already matches the substantive pattern a real commercial LLM gateway uses for its sub-processor list, but lacks an explicit per-provider cross-border-transfer-safeguard disclosure.** A real commercial LLM gateway (LLM Gateway) deliberately does not hardcode its AI-provider sub-processor list inside a static legal document, because a static list "would be out of date within days" — it instead points to a separately-maintained live table covering contracting entity, HQ/regions, retention window, training-use policy, and certifications per provider. This is structurally identical to what `PROVIDERS.md` already is for Kelvran: a living manifest ("kept current with Gateway's routing config") of provider/data-class/auth-mechanism/residency-notes. What that reference page has that `PROVIDERS.md` does not: an explicit, provider-by-provider disclosure of cross-border-transfer-safeguard status — including an explicit admission that SCCs are *not* executed for some providers headquartered in non-adequacy countries, and a disclaimer that no blanket Article 46 GDPR safeguard is being claimed. **`build_now`**: add a "Cross-border transfer safeguard" column/note to `PROVIDERS.md` per provider (SCC-in-place / adequacy-country / DPF-certified / not-independently-verified). **`not_yet`**: Kelvran itself signing DPAs/SCCs on any operator's behalf — not applicable; Kelvran is self-hosted infrastructure, not a data processor with its own customer contracts. *(Medium confidence — 2-1/3-0.)*

4. **GDPR "right to erasure" vs the semantic cache — real, directly-verified code gap: `cache.Cache` (`gateway/internal/cache/port.go`) has only `Get`/`Put`, no delete/purge-by-key or purge-by-tenant.** Direct read of `gateway/internal/cache/port.go` confirms the `Cache` interface Gateway's whole dataplane depends on exposes exactly two methods: `Get(ctx, key) (resp []byte, writtenAt time.Time, ok bool, err error)` and `Put(ctx, key, resp []byte, ttl time.Duration) error` — no `Delete`, no purge-by-key, no purge-by-tenant, no admin-triggerable eviction beyond passive TTL expiry per layer. `THREAT_MODEL.md`'s own NIST AI 600-1 crosswalk (Data Privacy row) already states PII guardrails scan every message role and tenant-namespace isolation is real, but says nothing about whether an already-cached entry containing PII can be surgically removed. Given the product's own design explicitly accepts that PII can traverse the system (a message that passes guardrail checks, or predates a guardrail rule, can still be cached), and given L1(exact)/L2(normalized)/L3(lexical near-duplicate) all persist responses up to their configured TTL, there is today no mechanism to service a GDPR Article 17 erasure request against a specific data subject's cached content except waiting out the TTL or flushing the entire cache (which destroys cache value for every other tenant too). Two web-sourced candidate claims that would have addressed this via a third-party tool ("Vismaran") were checked and **refuted 0-3 each** — no such external tool applies here; this finding rests entirely on direct source verification, not adversarially-voted web research. **This is the single most concrete, novel, currently-undocumented finding in this entire research pass.** **`build_now`**: add `Delete(ctx, key string) error` to the `Cache` interface plus the in-process implementation — small, additive, non-breaking. Document the interim manual operator procedure (reduce TTL / restart cache) in `SECURITY.md` or a new `docs/operations/DATA-SUBJECT-REQUESTS.md` until `Delete` ships. **`not_yet`**: a fully automated "find every cache entry that might belong to subject X" sweep — needs either a write-time PII-provenance index (a real design commitment, not a small addition) or an honest, stated limitation that erasure is scoped to "delete by exact request-key you can identify." *(High confidence — direct code verification, not a web vote.)*

5. **ISO/IEC 42001 & NIST AI RMF's Govern function — an organizational-certification framework, not a code artifact, so not something Kelvran-the-repo can "build"; but its Govern 6.1/6.2 third-party-AI-risk lens surfaces one real, currently-unstated documentation gap.** ISO/IEC 42001 scopes itself to organizations that either provide *or utilize* AI-based products/services — so conceptually an operator deploying Kelvran, not just an AI model builder, falls within its intended audience. But an AI Management System (AIMS) is defined as an organization-level system of policies/objectives/processes for governing AI use, backed by named leadership accountability and periodic management review (NIST's own crosswalk maps Govern 2.3 — executive accountability for AI-risk decisions — to ISO clauses 5.1/9.3.1-3) — a certifiable organizational-governance exercise requiring an operating entity, employees, and ongoing practices an auditor assesses, which an OSS repository with no legal entity behind it structurally cannot satisfy. **The one substantively new, checkable finding:** NIST's Govern 6.1/6.2 rows (third-party AI-risk policies; contingency processes for high-risk third-party data/AI-system failures) map to ISO 42001's B.10.2 (Allocating responsibilities) and B.10.3 (Suppliers) clauses — and Kelvran's entire purpose is routing to third-party AI providers, with real fallback-chain/circuit-breaker mechanics already shipped in code (a real `maxConsecutiveChainFailures` circuit breaker, health-probe-driven WRR routing, per this session's own chaos-engineering research) — yet neither `THREAT_MODEL.md` nor `SECURITY.md` currently frames or documents these as a Govern-function "third-party AI-provider risk/contingency" control; they're described only in reliability/DoS terms. A related candidate claim (that Govern 1.1 requires a formal AI Policy artifact distinct from risk-taxonomy work) was checked and **refuted 1-2**, so this report does not assert a formal-AI-Policy-document requirement. **`build_now`**: a short "Third-Party AI Provider Risk & Contingency" note in `SECURITY.md` or `THREAT_MODEL.md`, explicitly reframing the already-shipped fallback-chain/circuit-breaker/health-probing mechanics through this NIST-Govern-6.1/6.2 lens — reuses existing code, purely a documentation/framing addition, closes a real blind spot (`THREAT_MODEL.md`'s own crosswalk is Measure-function-shaped, not Govern-function-shaped). **`not_yet`**: actual ISO 42001 certification pursuit, or a formal AI Policy governance document — both require an operating organization with leadership and a management-review cadence to certify against; no operator has requested it. *(High confidence.)*
